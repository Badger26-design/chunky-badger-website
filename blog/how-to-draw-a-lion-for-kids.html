How to Draw a Lion
for Kids

🗓️ 18 October 2026
⏱️ 3 min read
✏️ Ages 4–9

Ask a child to name the most exciting animal in the world and there's a very good chance they'll roar back at you. Lions have everything a young artist loves — a big friendly face, a wild fluffy mane, and a tail with a little tuft on the end like a paintbrush. Best of all, a lion is built almost entirely from circles and ovals, which makes learning how to draw a lion for kids far easier than it looks.

This guide uses the same 6 Simple Steps method we use in every Chunky Badger book. We start with two basic shapes, add a few features, then finish with the fun bits — the mane, the whiskers and all that lovely golden colour. No tracing, no complicated instructions, and no need to be "good at art" to get a lion you'll want to stick on the fridge. 🦁

What You'll Need

  • ✏️ A pencil (an HB is perfect)
  • 🧹 An eraser
  • ✏️ A sharpener
  • 🎨 Coloured pencils or felt tips (yellow, orange, brown and black)
  • 📄 A sheet of plain paper

That's genuinely it. One of the nicest things about drawing is that the whole kit fits in a pencil case.

How to Draw a Lion in 6 Simple Steps

1

Step 1: Draw the Head and Body

Start with a circle near the top of your paper for the head. Just below it and a little to the right, draw a bigger rounded shape for the body, letting the two overlap slightly. That overlap is what stops your lion looking like a snowman.

Keep your pencil strokes nice and light — these first shapes are only a guide, and light lines are much easier to rub out later!
2

Step 2: Add the Mane and Ears

Draw a wavy, scalloped ring all the way around the head, like the petals of a big flower. Then add two little ears poking up out of the mane, each with a smaller curve tucked inside. This is the step that turns a cat into a lion.

Try to keep the bumps around the mane roughly the same size as each other — it looks much tidier than a mix of big and small.
3

Step 3: Add the Face

Draw a curved line inside the mane to mark out the face. Then add two round eyes, a small rounded nose in the middle, and a curved mouth underneath shaped like a tiny letter w.

Put the nose in first and the eyes either side of it. Starting in the middle keeps everything evenly spaced.
4

Step 4: Add the Legs

Time to stand your lion up. Draw two front legs coming straight down from under the mane, and two back legs behind them. Finish each one with a soft rounded paw and a couple of little toe lines.

Lion legs are chunky and strong, not thin and spindly. Make them a bit thicker than you think you need to.
5

Step 5: Add the Tail

Draw a long curved line sweeping up and out from the back of the body. At the very end, add a pointed tuft shaped a bit like a little flame.

Curve the tail upwards and your lion looks proud and happy. Let it droop down and he looks sleepy — try both and see which you prefer!
6

Step 6: Add the Finishing Touches

Last few details and you're done. Add three whiskers on each side of the face, a little zigzag of fluffy fur on the chest, and two soft rosy cheeks. Rub out any guide lines you don't need, go over the lines you're keeping, and your lion is ready to colour.

There's a "Draw and Colour" space on the right of every sheet in the book — perfect for having a second go without starting a new page.

Your Lion Colouring Guide

  • Body: a warm golden amber
  • Mane and tail tuft: a deeper orange-brown, darker than the body so it stands out
  • Muzzle, chest fluff and inner ears: a soft creamy white
  • Nose: black, with a cream muzzle around it
  • Eyes: simple black dots — no need for anything fancy
  • Cheeks: a touch of soft pink
Body: golden amber
Mane & tail tuft: orange-brown
Muzzle & chest: creamy white
Nose & eyes: black

For an extra-professional finish, colour in the same direction all over — it makes the whole drawing look tidier. And if your child wants a purple lion with spots, let them have a purple lion with spots. Confidence first, realism later.

Well Done, Little Artist! 🎉

You've just drawn a lion from nothing but a couple of circles — that's a real skill, and it gets easier every single time. If your young artist enjoyed this one, try changing just one thing on the next attempt: a bigger mane, a lion lying down, or a whole pride of them across one big sheet of paper. Repetition with small changes is how children build confidence fastest.
